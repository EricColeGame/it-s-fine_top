## What Is It's Fine? A Crunch Simulator With a Dog for a Boss

It's Fine is an idle/incremental game about developing your own game. You write code, buy upgrades, and prestige your way toward a stable build, all while Joe — your dog, your boss, and your unrelenting Project Manager — demands results. The clock is the hook: instead of an infinite treadmill, the fiction locks you into the final 24 hours before delivery.

The game is available on Steam, where the idle/incremental tag is crowded with spreadsheets dressed up as clickers. What separates It's Fine is tone. Crunch culture is a real, well-documented problem in game development, and the It's Fine developer treats it as both a joke and a critique — you are the punchline, and Joe is the deadline made flesh.

## The Core Loop: Code, Upgrade, Prestige

Strip away the dog and the deadline, and It's Fine runs on the classic incremental skeleton — but each stage has a thematic twist that shapes your decisions. The It's Fine developer leans on the fiction to justify why you reset, why you lose progress, and why starting over is framed as "shipping a stable version" rather than failure.

### Prestige Layers and Why They Matter

Multi-layered prestige is the backbone of the mid and late game. A single reset layer gets stale fast — you learn the optimal path and repeat it. Layered prestige keeps reintroducing new variables, so each reset changes what "efficient" means.

Practically, that means you should treat each reset as a research trip. Note which upgrades carried you, which became dead weight, and which new mechanic appeared after the reset. If you are used to idle games where prestige is a simple multiplier, the extra layers here are where the real learning curve lives. Community reports consistently point to prestige timing as the single biggest factor separating a fast run from a slow one.

## Tips for Surviving Your First Crunch Run

New players tend to make the same three mistakes: hoarding, resetting too early, and ignoring hidden content. Here is a cleaner approach.

- **Automate before you optimize.** Buying the upgrade that removes manual clicking beats buying a bigger multiplier, because idle games reward time, not attention.
- **Read the tooltip, then read it again.** Prestige descriptions often hint at what unlocks next.
- **Reset with a goal.** Ask what you want the next run to prove, not just how many multipliers you can stack.
- **Sweep for secrets on every run.** Hidden content is usually gated behind specific states, not raw progress.
- **Keep notes.** A one-line log of what changed after each reset saves hours later.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy automation early | Hoard currency for one giant purchase |
| Reset with a specific goal | Reset the instant the button appears |
| Read patch and wiki notes | Assume hidden content is unreachable |
| Track what each reset unlocks | Blame RNG for a slow run |
| Check the Discord when stuck | Grind blindly for hours first |

## Why the Crunch Setting Actually Works

Crunch is a subject most games avoid, because depicting it risks glamorizing it. The It's Fine developer sidesteps that by making the deadline absurd. Joe is not a villain; he is a dog who wants the build shipped. The pressure is real, the stakes are ridiculous, and that gap is the joke.

It also gives the incremental genre something it usually lacks: an ending. Idle games are famous for never concluding. Here, the fiction hands you a finish line, and the alternate endings give you reasons to cross it more than once.

If you enjoy optimization, layered progression, and a bit of workplace humor, the premise alone is worth a look. If you have ever shipped anything under a deadline, it will land harder.
