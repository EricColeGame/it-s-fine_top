## Why the It's Fine Progression Feels Unsettled Yet Bright

Most pop and alt-rock songs pick a key and stay there. "It's Fine (Every Time)" does the opposite. According to the crowd-maintained TheoryTab analysis, the song travels through **F♯ major**, **D major**, and **B major** — three keys that are close cousins rather than strangers.

That relationship matters. D major and B minor are relative keys, and F♯ major is the dominant of B minor. In other words, all three tonal centers live in the same neighborhood. The modulation never feels like a jarring jump; it feels like walking into the next room.

| Key | Relationship to the others | Sections that use it |

| F♯ major | Dominant of B minor; shares most notes with B major | Intro, Verse, Outro |
| D major | Relative major of B minor | Chorus |
| B major | Closely related to F♯ major (one note apart) | Bridge |

Because the keys are linked by thirds and fourths rather than by blunt semitone shifts, the transitions sound smooth on the surface while the underlying mood keeps changing. That tension between smoothness and instability is the engine of the whole track.

## Section-by-Section: How the It's Fine Progression Moves

Each section of the song has its own tonal home, its own tempo nuance, and its own melodic range. Community reports on Hooktheory list the tempo as hovering right around 109 BPM in 4/4, with tiny variations between sections — a human, breathing feel rather than a rigid grid.

### Intro and Verse: Grounding in F♯ Major

Both the intro and the verse sit in F♯ major, and both lean most heavily on the tonic chord (I). That's the song's home base. The verse melody stays inside a tight F♯3–F♯4 octave, which keeps the vocal delivery conversational and low-key. Nothing in the verse is trying to impress you — it's setting a trap.

### Chorus: A Shift to D Major

The chorus lands in D major, and the most-used chord is reported as the flattened seventh (♭VII). In D major, that chord is C major — a chord borrowed from outside the strict major scale. Borrowed chords like this are a classic way to make a chorus feel wider and slightly darker without tipping into full minor. The melody drops to F♯3–D4, which gives the chorus a heavier, more grounded weight than the verse.

### Bridge: Lifting into B Major

The bridge moves to B major and, unusually, keeps the tonic (I) as its most-used chord. It also pushes the melody up to C♯4–C♯5 — the highest register in the song. Tempo nudges up to about 111 BPM. Everything about the bridge says "lift," which is why it functions as the emotional peak rather than a simple contrast section.

### Outro: Back Home, With a Twist

The outro returns to F♯ major but centers on the vi chord (D♯ minor) instead of the tonic. Ending on a minor chord in a major key leaves the listener hanging — a deliberate choice for a song whose entire lyrical premise is pretending everything is fine.

## How to Use These Ideas in Your Own Songs

You don't need three key changes to borrow from this track. Start with one technique at a time.

- **Modulate to a closely related key.** Move from your verse key to its relative major or minor, or to the dominant. The shift will register emotionally without confusing the listener.
- **Borrow a ♭VII chord in a major chorus.** In D major, that's C major. It adds grit while staying easy to play.
- **Save your highest melody note for the bridge.** The It's Fine progression keeps the chorus mid-range and lets the bridge climb — that's why the bridge feels like a release.
- **End on the vi chord.** A minor chord closing a major-key song leaves a question mark hanging, which suits ambivalent lyrics.
- **Let the melody be simpler than the chords.** Stepwise motion and repeated notes give listeners something to hold onto while the harmony does the surprising work.

| Minor-chord ending | Outro (vi) | Beginner |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What key is "It's Fine (Every Time)" in?**
The song doesn't stay in one key. Community-sourced analysis lists F♯ major for the intro, verse, and outro, D major for the chorus, and B major for the bridge. That three-key structure is the defining feature of the It's Fine progression.

**Why does the song sound tense if it's mostly in major keys?**
Major keys don't automatically sound happy. The tension comes from borrowed chords, a melody that lands on chord tones only about 57% of the time, and an outro that ends on the minor vi chord instead of resolving home.

**Is this progression hard to play on guitar or piano?**
Not especially. The individual chords are common shapes. The challenge is remembering which key you're in as the song moves between sections, so practice the transitions slowly before playing along at full tempo.

**Can I use a similar approach in my own songwriting?**
Absolutely. Start by modulating to a relative key, borrow one chord from outside the scale, and let your melody stay simple while the harmony gets interesting. Even one of those moves will make your progression feel less predictable.

The takeaway is simple: "It's Fine (Every Time)" earns its emotional ambiguity through craft, not accident. Every key change, borrowed chord, and hovering melody note is doing a job — and once you can name those jobs, you can put them to work in your own music.
